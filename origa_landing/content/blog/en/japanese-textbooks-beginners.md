# Japanese Textbooks for Beginners: Minna no Nihongo, Genki, and the Alternatives

Apps cover vocabulary and review, but sequential grammar with graded exercises still comes from a textbook. The problem is that picking your first one means wading through fan wars with few honest comparisons.

Below is a breakdown of five options without a "best of the best" rating: each carries its own trade-off, and the right choice depends on three things — your intermediary language, budget, and study format.

## Minna no Nihongo

The standard of Japanese language schools worldwide. The main text is entirely in Japanese from lesson one — but within the kana you've learned.

Strengths: strict structure, JLPT-level alignment, enormous exercise volume, decades of polish. One caveat worth knowing upfront: grammar explanations come in a **separate translation volume** — effectively doubling your budget. The main book alone is of little use for self-study.

Best for: learners ready for systematic study who want a clear lesson → exercise sequence.

## Genki I–II

The American university standard. Everything in one volume: dialogues, English grammar explanations, exercises, culture notes.

Strengths: no supplementary books needed, thorough explanations, modern vocabulary. Weaknesses: pricier and heavier, pace assumes a classroom, some exercises are pair-based and need adapting for solo study.

Best for: those comfortable with English as the intermediary language who want maximum context in one place.

## Tae Kim's Grammar Guide

A free electronic grammar reference. Not a course in the strict sense — few exercises, but exceptionally clear explanations of why the language works the way it does.

Available in print and free online. Best for: supplementing any main textbook when you want the "why," not just the "how." As a sole resource — not enough practice.

## Irodori

A free online course from the Japan Foundation focused on everyday communication. Modern communicative methodology, audio included, levels from Introductory to Elementary A2/B1. Weakness: less grammatical system than Minna/Genki — more conversation scenarios, fewer drills.

Best for: speaking ASAP on a zero budget. Word sets from Irodori import into Origa as ready-made decks ([the content page](/content)).

## How to Choose

1. **Intermediary language.** If your English is shaky, Genki turns Japanese study into English study.
2. **Budget.** Minna requires two volumes; Genki is one expensive tome; Tae Kim and Irodori are free.
3. **Format.** Need written exercises — Minna/Genki. Need to understand the system — Tae Kim. Need to speak within months — Irodori.
4. **Check progress migration.** Textbook words should go into an SRS app (and they should) — make sure the decks import ready-made rather than by hand.

One textbook plus SRS beats three textbooks without a system — see [the zero-to-start plan](/blog/yaponskiy-s-nulya).

## How to Work Through a Textbook

A textbook pays off only with the right lesson cycle:

1. **The lesson dialogue** — read aloud without peeking at the translation. Not understanding yet is fine.
2. **Grammar notes** — read them; write out one example sentence per rule.
3. **Exercises** — in writing, checked against the key. Mentally "answering" doesn't count: your hand catches errors your eye misses.
4. **Lesson vocabulary into an SRS app** the same day. Words without review vanish faster than you reach the next lesson.
5. **Re-read the dialogue** after exercises — it should now make sense end to end.

Pace: one lesson every 2–3 days at 30–60 minutes daily. Minna no Nihongo's 50 lessons take 4–6 months — which is exactly the natural runway into N4 territory.

Paper or digital is habit — but for Japanese, paper has an edge: margins for handwritten kanji notes and no browser-tab temptation.

## Limitations of Origa

Origa is not a textbook and replaces none of the courses above: it doesn't walk you through grammar lesson by lesson. Its role is companion work — reinforcing vocabulary and kanji from these textbooks through spaced repetition, furigana while reading, and listening practice. ## Bottom Line

For most self-learners the working stack looks like this: Minna no Nihongo as the main course (or Genki if your English is strong), Tae Kim as the "why" reference, and an SRS app as the retention engine. ## FAQ

### Can I skip the textbook entirely?

For the first six months, maybe — then you'll hit a wall: apps give vocabulary and review but not sequential grammar. A cheap compromise: free Tae Kim plus Irodori.

### Which textbook is the best?

Whichever you actually finish. All else equal, Minna no Nihongo is the most proven route out the other end into N4 territory.

### Do I need the workbooks?

For handwriting practice, yes. For comprehension and reading, not really — the main volume's exercises suffice.
