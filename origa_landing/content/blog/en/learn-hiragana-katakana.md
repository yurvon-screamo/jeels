# Hiragana and Katakana: Learning Both Scripts in Two Weeks

The first wall in Japanese isn't kanji. It's what stands in front of them: two syllabaries, 46 basic characters each. Beginners stall here for months — not because the material is hard, but because they learn it wrong: passively, one script at a time, with no review system.

Two weeks is a realistic timeframe to recognize both scripts fluently, at 30–45 minutes a day. Here's a day-by-day plan, the methods that work, and the mistakes that stretch the process into half a year.

## Why Japanese Has Two Scripts

It's historical legacy, not spite. **Hiragana** writes grammatical elements and native Japanese words: particles like は and を, verb conjugations, words like たべる (to eat). **Katakana** is used for loanwords: コーヒー (koohii — coffee), テレビ (terebi — TV), スマホ (sumaho — smartphone), plus onomatopoeia and emphasis — roughly what italics do in English.

The split is practical: when you see a katakana character mid-sentence, you already know the word is likely foreign. That's a reading aid — and it's exactly what you lose if you only learn hiragana.

Each script has 46 core characters plus derived forms with voicing (か → が) and combinations (きゃ、しゅ). The derived forms follow rules from the basics, so the real memorization load is 92 characters.

## What "Knowing a Script" Means

Three different levels that often get conflated:

1. **Recognition.** You see a character and recall its sound. Enough to read with a dictionary.
2. **Production.** You hear a syllable and write the character. Needed for handwriting.
3. **Speed.** You read text without pausing to recall characters. Needed to enjoy reading.

The plan below targets level one in two weeks. Level two comes with writing practice; level three comes only from reading real texts, which is fine — nobody reads Japanese fluently after a month.

## The 14-Day Plan

### Days 1–5: Hiragana

One table row per day: あ行 (a-i-u-e-o), then か行, さ行, and so on — 5–8 new characters daily.

- Write each new character 5–10 times while saying the sound out loud. The tracing isn't for beautiful handwriting; it forces attention to shape.
- At the end of each day, read aloud every character learned since day one. Recall right before forgetting is the main retention mechanism.
- Set up spaced-repetition cards immediately — not "after I've learned them."

### Days 6–10: Katakana

Same row-by-row order, with one addition: start each session with five minutes of hiragana reading. Katakana sticks worse precisely because early texts use it less — your brain has nothing to anchor on, so deliberate review matters even more than it did for hiragana.

### Days 11–14: Mixed Reading

Spend four days reading real short texts where both scripts mix. Loanwords are ideal material: they're written in katakana, but you already know most of them by sound — コーヒー (coffee), カメラ (camera), テーブル (table). Guessing by sound trains both scripts at once.

Checkpoint: a table of random characters from both scripts, read aloud in 3–4 minutes with no more than 3–5 errors. If slower, add a couple of days of reading. Normal.

## Methods: What Works and What Doesn't

**Spaced repetition — yes.** Cards shown right before forgetting beat re-reading tables by a wide margin. Looking at a table once works not at all.

**Tracing — in moderation.** 5–10 repetitions per character help attention. A 50-page notebook per character wastes time when the goal is recognition.

**Mnemonics — carefully.** Stories like "き looks like a key" speed up initial encoding but slow the transition to direct recognition: you'll mentally replay the story instead of just seeing the character. Use them for stubborn characters only, not all of them.

**Apps with scheduling — yes.** Anything that removes manual tracking of "what to review today" saves the one resource beginners lack: consistency.

## Common Mistakes

**Learning hiragana and abandoning katakana.** The classic: hiragana goes smoothly, katakana feels optional. A month later you open a menu or packaging and understand nothing. Katakana catches up with you in week three either way; the only question is whether you learned it deliberately or reconstruct it painfully from context.

**Learning characters outside words.** A character memorized in isolation fades faster than one seen inside a word you can already say.

**Handwriting perfectionism.** At the recognition stage, shape matters more than calligraphy. Beautiful penmanship is a separate skill for later.

**"Perfect mastery before moving on."** The kana aren't a final exam; they're a tool. 90% recognition is enough to start reading simple texts where the remaining 10% takes care of itself.

## FAQ

### Can I learn both scripts simultaneously?

You can, but the characters will interfere. Sequential — hiragana first, then katakana — is psychologically easier and takes the same two weeks. One rule: once you start katakana, keep reviewing hiragana daily.

### How many characters per day is realistic?

5–8 new ones, provided you review all previous ones. More only if you have 2+ hours a day, and retention drops anyway.

### Do I need handwriting practice?

For recognition, no — 5–10 traces per character suffice for attention. For writing, yes, but that's a separate skill you can postpone for months.

### What should I read after learning both scripts?

A textbook — kana appear there in a controlled order. From real-world content: captions, menus, signs — short forms with a high share of loanwords. Full texts with furigana come after your first few hundred words; more on that in [learning Japanese through manga](/blog/learn-japanese-from-manga).
